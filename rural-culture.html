Rural Culture

Roots and Realities

During my master’s program at Pacific University, Dr. Lisa Christiansen encouraged me to pursue an independent study on how stereotypes about rural communities can shape behavioral health care. I wanted to understand what clinicians might overlook when they assume they know why a client is reluctant to seek help or what kind of support the client would trust.

I reviewed the literature on rural mental health and developed teaching materials for a Cultural Foundations course. That work became Roots and Realities, a poster I presented at the 2025 Western Psychological Association convention. It introduced three lenses for understanding rural life in a clinical setting.

Three lenses for understanding rural culture

The three lenses move from the broad identities people use to introduce themselves to the routines and expectations that are specific to a person or family.

Lens 01

Primary identity

The first lens considers the broad groups a person identifies with. These may include racial, ethnic, religious, or political identities such as White, Latino, Native American, Christian, or conservative. They are often among the first affiliations a person names when describing who they are. They are also the kinds of categories used to describe populations in surveys and demographic research. In a clinical conversation, the question is which of these identities the person emphasizes and what belonging to those groups means to them.

Lens 02

Duty and work

The second lens examines how a person’s roles and responsibilities shape what they do. Occupation is part of that picture, alongside school, caregiving, and obligations to family or neighbors. Place matters, too. Living on a farm, working in town, or moving between the two can bring different routines and expectations. During harvest, for example, a person’s availability may depend on the weather, the work still to be done, and who else is counting on them.

Lens 03

Subtle norms

The third lens focuses on the most personal layer of culture. Some practices are particular to a family or a small circle of people. Others are so familiar to the people who live by them that they rarely come up in conversation. A family might show concern by bringing food or taking over chores, while leaving it to the person who is struggling to bring up how they feel. Those everyday habits help a clinician understand what support looks like in that household, who is trusted with private concerns, and how someone signals that they need help.
